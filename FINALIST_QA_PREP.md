# Vanguard Open finalist Q&A preparation

This document is private preparation for a possible ten-minute authorship and reasoning interview.
It does not modify the submitted entry or PDF. Answers should be delivered conversationally and
only from claims already supported by the submission.

## Thirty-second opening

My project asks where classroom AI should stop. I accept AI when a learner deliberately invokes it
to test ideas, translate, generate counterexamples, or check evidence. I refuse silent inference
about attention, effort, emotion, honesty, or motivation from behavioral traces. The design makes
that boundary physical: a private Ephemeral Workbench, a shared Evidence Table for chosen claims,
and an Unscored Commons with no analytics. The point is not less AI; it is keeping AI powerful where
its output can be inspected and contestable, while refusing it authority over a student's inner
state.

## Likely questions and answer anchors

### 1. Why attention inference specifically?

- It is attractive because it promises early intervention, but it converts ambiguous traces—gaze,
  pauses, clicks, typing, speech, or silence—into claims about a person.
- The inference is hard to inspect or appeal and can punish neurodivergent, multilingual, anxious,
  or culturally atypical learners.
- It changes behavior: students learn to perform what the model recognizes as engagement instead
  of taking intellectual risks.

### 2. Is this simply an anti-surveillance policy?

- No. The three spaces make the refusal operational rather than aspirational.
- The Workbench controls context and retention; the Evidence Table records selected public
  reasoning; the Commons removes instrumentation entirely.
- Remove the refusal and the design stops functioning as intended: privacy, group reasoning, and
  unscored exploration all change.

### 3. What does AI actually improve?

- Learner-requested counterexamples, practice variants, translations, alternate representations,
  accessibility, arithmetic checks, and source-grounded questions.
- Group-level contradiction detection, citation-to-claim checking, and adversarial questions.
- Clerical work such as formatting and transcription, freeing teachers for dialogue and judgment.

### 4. What about a student who needs help but never asks?

- This is the strongest cost of rejecting predictive dashboards.
- Replace hidden inference with visible support: short human check-ins, stable assistance menus,
  explicit help signals, accommodations, and structural facts such as missing access or work.
- The replacement is slower, but its meaning is defined and contestable rather than guessed.

### 5. Would anything be retained for safety?

- Limited system events may be retained for service failure, malware, or an explicit abuse report.
- Retention must have a stated purpose, expiry date, and appeal path.
- Do not turn safety logging into content-derived personality or engagement profiling.

### 6. How can a teacher assess learning without participation scores?

- Inspect actual claims, sources, revisions, rejected suggestions, and documented uncertainty.
- Ask why a claim survived and what evidence would change it.
- A quiet correction of a false premise may be more valuable than visible activity; traces are not
  equivalent to contribution.

### 7. Is the Unscored Commons realistic under curriculum pressure?

- It has a real opportunity cost, and that is part of the position.
- A weekly protected period treats exploration as infrastructure, not a reward after “real work.”
- Schools can evaluate the program through sampled work and student feedback without instrumenting
  every minute inside it.

### 8. How would you test whether this classroom works?

- Audit whether claims become better supported and whether students can explain revisions.
- Check accessibility, help-request response time, and whether different learners can use or refuse
  AI without penalty.
- Interview students about agency and chilling effects.
- Publish what cannot be validly inferred instead of substituting a convenient attention score.

### 9. What is the hardest counterargument?

- A well-calibrated early-warning model might catch a struggling learner whom a teacher misses.
- The response is not that the benefit is impossible; it is that uncertain psychological inference
  can become authoritative before a learner can contest it.
- The design deliberately pays a speed cost for explicit, human-readable support signals.

### 10. What would make you revise the refusal?

- Better predictive accuracy alone is insufficient.
- Any proposal would need a learner-controlled purpose, visible inputs and outputs, meaningful
  consent without penalty, a practical appeal process, strict expiry, and proof that less invasive
  support cannot meet the need.
- Even then, character or motivation judgments should not enter a permanent student record.

## Authorship verification

- Core design choice: distinguish intentional learner disclosures from silent system inference.
- Three-space structure: Workbench for private experimentation, Evidence Table for chosen public
  reasoning, Commons for uninstrumented curiosity.
- Central sentence to explain in original words: AI may help students test ideas; it may not
  secretly test students for inner traits.
- AI use was disclosed: Codex helped interpret the rubric, explore structures, pressure-test
  objections, and edit prose. The entrant chose the refusal, selected the design, and accepted the
  final text.
- No student data, testimonials, empirical efficacy claims, or generated images are present.
